    Short answer

    Clinics seeking international patients in Korea must register to attract foreign patients, keep every ad within the Medical Service Act (no misleading patient testimonials or comparisons, pre-review on designated media), and build presence where patients actually look: YouTube, Xiaohongshu and Google abroad, Naver, Instagram, KakaoTalk and Korean review apps at home. Korea drew 2.01 million foreign patients in 2025, the first time above two million (MOHW).

    Korea welcomed 2.01 million foreign patients in 2025 — the first year above two million — and dermatology accounted for 62.9% of them (Ministry of Health and Welfare, April 2026). For clinics, K-beauty groups and medical-tourism agencies the opportunity is real, but the legal layer (the Medical Service Act and foreign-patient registration) and the discovery stack (Korean review apps, Xiaohongshu, KakaoTalk) differ sharply from the US healthcare playbook. This guide covers both.

    1. Korea's Medical Tourism Market — Who Comes and Why

    Korea's foreign patients came from 201 countries in 2025. China and Japan together made up 60.6%, followed by Taiwan (9.2%), the United States (8.6%) and Thailand (2.9%); patients from the US rose 70.4% from the year before. Dermatology was the largest department, with 1.31 million patients (62.9%).

    Three advantages explain the pull: a dense supply of specialist clinics in Seoul, prices that international patients often find competitive with home, and the cultural reach of K-beauty, K-pop and K-drama. Each origin market discovers clinics differently, so the channel mix has to follow the nationality mix (§3–4).

    2. Legal Framework — Korean Medical Advertising Law (의료법)

    The biggest mistake foreign clinics make in Korea is treating medical marketing like consumer marketing. Article 56 of the Medical Service Act bans, among other things:

    • Misleading patient testimonials — treatment experiences presented in a way that could mislead about the effect of treatment
    • Comparisons with other providers — "better than clinic X" style claims
    • Exaggeration and unverifiable claims — "best in Korea", "100% safe", "guaranteed result"

    Violating Article 56(1)–(3) can lead to up to one year in prison or a fine of up to ₩10 million (Article 89). Medical ads on media designated by the Enforcement Decree must be pre-reviewed before they run, by the review committees of the medical, dental or Korean-medicine associations (Article 57).

    For international-patient marketing, the practical rule: review every Korean-language asset against these rules before publishing, and apply the same honesty standard to English, Chinese, Japanese and Thai assets — they are often seen by people in Korea through global platforms. Have any price or discount messaging reviewed by Korean counsel, and build review time into the launch plan.

    3. Patient Acquisition Funnel — Inbound vs Korea-Resident Foreigner

    The inbound funnel serves patients flying in from overseas. They decide well in advance, compare several Korean clinics and combine treatment with travel. Discovery runs through YouTube vlogs, Xiaohongshu (RedNote) for Chinese patients and Google search for most others; the decision usually happens in a KakaoTalk or WhatsApp consultation with photos and a quote.

    The Korea-resident foreigner funnel is shorter. Expats, students and long-term workers behave more like Korean patients — searching Naver in mixed Korean and English, browsing Instagram and KakaoTalk channels, and reading Korean review apps — and expect an in-person consultation.

    The two funnels share a clinic but need different content, languages and coordinators, so plan them as separate workstreams. Clinics with an existing brand abroad usually start with the inbound funnel.

    4. K-Beauty Clinic Discovery — How Patients Find Clinics

    • GangnamUnni (강남언니) and BabiTalk (바비톡) — Korean clinic-review and price apps that Korea-resident patients use to compare clinics and procedures.
    • Xiaohongshu / RedNote (小红书) — a primary discovery channel for Chinese patients; run it with a native Chinese-speaking team.
    • YouTube — long-form experience vlogs that international patients watch before choosing a clinic.
    • Naver and Instagram — for Korea-resident patients and for checking a clinic's Korean reputation.

    Two operational details separate clinics that convert. First, multilingual coordinators who answer quickly on KakaoTalk and WhatsApp in the patient's language. Second, real reviews: reviews on review apps belong to the patients who wrote them — never write, buy or edit them, and remember that clinic ads cannot use misleading treatment testimonials under Article 56.

    5. Pricing & Package Strategy for International Patients

    International patients respond to clearly bundled packages rather than à-la-carte pricing: a patient flying in wants treatment, recovery accommodation, check-ups and ground transport explained up front. Three tiers — Essential, Recovery and Full-face — keep the choice simple.

    Show what each tier includes, state the currency and exchange-rate basis, and publish the deposit and cancellation terms in the contract. Prices vary widely by procedure and clinic, so quote case by case after a consultation rather than advertising discounts.

    6. Compliance Stack & 90-Day Launch for Foreign Clinic Marketing

    Registration. Under Article 6 of the Act on Support for Overseas Expansion of Medical Services and Attraction of Foreign Patients, clinics and agencies that solicit foreign patients must register. Agencies need, among other things, at least ₩100 million in capital (with exceptions for registered general travel agencies), guarantee insurance and an office in Korea; registration is valid for three years. Attracting foreign patients without registration can be punished by up to three years in prison or a ₩30 million fine.

    Documents before the first ad. Prepare (1) translated explanations and written-consent forms for surgery and similar procedures (Medical Service Act Article 24-2), (2) a price sheet showing what is included, and (3) translated post-op care instructions in every language you market to.

    Agencies. If you work with medical-tourism agencies to bootstrap inbound flow, use registered agencies only, treat them as a paid acquisition channel with clear KPIs, and keep building your own channels alongside them.

    The 90-day sequence. Days 1–14 registration and legal review; Days 15–30 multilingual coordinators and agency contracts; Days 31–60 content production and medical-ad pre-review; Days 61–90 channel launch and paid amplification.

    FAQ

    What are the rules for medical advertising in Korea?

    Article 56 of the Medical Service Act bans, among other things, patient testimonials that could mislead about treatment effects, comparisons with other providers and exaggerated claims. Violating Article 56(1)–(3) can lead to up to one year in prison or a fine of up to ₩10 million (Article 89), and medical ads on media designated by the Enforcement Decree must be pre-reviewed by the associations' review committees before they run.

    Do clinics need to register to attract foreign patients in Korea?

    Yes. Under Article 6 of the Act on Support for Overseas Expansion of Medical Services and Attraction of Foreign Patients, clinics and agencies that solicit foreign patients must register; registration is valid for three years. Attracting foreign patients without registration can be punished by up to three years in prison or a ₩30 million fine. Agencies need, among other things, at least ₩100 million in capital, guarantee insurance and an office in Korea.

    Where do international patients find Korean clinics?

    Patients flying in from abroad mostly discover clinics through YouTube experience vlogs, Xiaohongshu (for Chinese patients) and Google, and decide in KakaoTalk or WhatsApp consultations. Foreigners living in Korea behave more like Korean patients — Naver, Instagram, KakaoTalk and Korean review apps such as GangnamUnni and BabiTalk.
