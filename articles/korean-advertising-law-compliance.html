    Short answer

    Korean advertising law applies to foreign brands whenever an ad is in Korean or directed at Korean audiences. The KFTC enforces the Act on Fair Labeling and Advertising and its endorsement guidelines, MFDS-administered laws cover food, health and cosmetics claims, medical ads need pre-review on designated media, and since December 1, 2024 paid posts must disclose the relationship in the title or opening.

    Korean advertising law is more layered than most US legal teams expect. Several bodies make or apply the rules — the KFTC, MFDS, the medical associations' review committees, the KMCC and the Korea Internet Advertising Foundation — and category rules sit on top of the general ban on false or misleading ads. This guide covers who does what, the endorsement rules, category rules, common violations, disclosure placement and a workflow that keeps campaigns compliant.

    1. The Korean Advertising Law Stack — Who Does What

    The Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) is the main enforcer for general consumer advertising. Article 3 of the Act on Fair Labeling and Advertising bans false or exaggerated, deceptive, unfairly comparative and disparaging ads, and the KFTC's endorsement guidelines set out how sponsored content must be disclosed. The Korea Media and Communications Commission (KMCC), which replaced the Korea Communications Commission in October 2025, oversees broadcasting and communications.

    The Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS, 식약처) administers the labeling and advertising rules for food, health functional food, drugs, cosmetics and medical devices. Medical advertising is reviewed in advance by committees run by the medical, dental and Korean-medicine associations when it runs on media designated by decree. The Korea Internet Advertising Foundation (KIAF, 한국인터넷광고재단) runs online-ad self-regulation and monitoring.

    The most expensive misunderstanding among foreign brands is "the advertiser is US-based, so US law applies." An ad in Korean, or directed at Korean audiences, is judged under Korean rules, and running it on a global platform (Instagram, YouTube) does not change that.

    2. KFTC Endorsement Rules — the December 2024 Update (추천·보증 심사지침)

    The KFTC's Recommendation and Endorsement Review Guidelines (추천·보증 등에 관한 표시·광고 심사지침) require anyone with an economic relationship to the advertiser to disclose it. The amendment in force since December 1, 2024 tightened where the disclosure must appear:

    • Text posts such as blogs: in the title or the opening of the post
    • Photo posts: inside the image, distinguishable from the background, or on the first line of the text
    • Video: in the title, or at the start and the end of the video; live streams repeat it
    • Not enough: a disclosure hidden among many hashtags or only visible after tapping "more"
    • Wording: name the benefit plainly — '광고' (advertisement), '협찬' (sponsorship), '상품 무상 제공' (product provided free)

    Free products, discounts, affiliate commissions and paid reposts are all economic relationships, so they need a disclosure too. Since June 1, 2026, ads that use AI-generated virtual people must also disclose that they are virtual.

    Enforcement and penalties. KFTC sanctions are aimed at the advertisers: in 2019 it issued corrective orders and fines totalling ₩269 million to seven advertisers whose Instagram influencer posts hid the sponsorship. Under the Act on Fair Labeling and Advertising, the administrative fine can reach 2% of the related sales (up to ₩500 million where sales cannot be calculated), and a violation of Article 3(1) can be punished by up to two years in prison or a fine of up to ₩150 million (Article 17). The KFTC and the Korea Consumer Agency also publish annual SNS monitoring results — the 2024 round was announced in March 2025.

    The operational rule: every Korean influencer contract includes the required disclosure wording and position, and every creator submission is checked by a Korean-speaking reviewer before it goes live.

    3. Industry-Specific Rules and Pre-Review

    Medical and clinics. Article 56 of the Medical Service Act bans, among other things, patient testimonials that could mislead about treatment effects and comparisons with other providers; violating Article 56(1)–(3) can lead to up to one year in prison or a fine of up to ₩10 million (Article 89). Medical ads on media designated by the Enforcement Decree must be pre-reviewed before they run.

    Cosmetics. Functional claims such as whitening or wrinkle improvement depend on the product itself passing MFDS functional-cosmetics review or reporting, and the ad must match those claims. Cosmetics may not be advertised in ways that make them look like medicines.

    Health functional food (건강기능식품). Under Article 10 of the Act on Labeling and Advertising of Foods, health functional food ads go through prior review by a self-regulatory body registered with MFDS (the Korea Health Supplements Association). Functional claims must match recognized functional ingredients, so claims that are routine in the US may not be usable in Korea.

    Subscriptions and e-commerce. The amended E-Commerce Act, in force since February 14, 2025, regulates six dark-pattern types: hidden renewals, drip pricing, pre-selected options, false hierarchy, obstructing cancellation and repeated nagging. Raising a subscription price or converting a free trial to paid requires additional consent and notice (Article 13(6)).

    4. Common Violations — Where Foreign Brands Trip

    • Undisclosed influencer collaborations — a free product plus a brief is an economic relationship; the post needs a disclosure in the required position
    • Disclosures in the wrong place — a tag buried among hashtags or behind "more" does not meet the December 2024 rules
    • Claims you cannot prove — saying a product is "MFDS-approved" when only one ingredient is recognized, or implying a patent from a pending application, risks being treated as false or exaggerated advertising under Article 3
    • Dark-pattern UX — free trials that roll into paid plans without additional consent, pre-checked options and hard-to-find cancellation flows are regulated under the E-Commerce Act

    Two systemic patterns repeat. First, reusing US disclosure language ("This is a paid partnership") on Korean-language posts instead of plain Korean wording. Second, relying on a creator agency's standard contract without checking that its disclosure clauses match the current guidelines.

    5. Disclosure Language — How to Do It Right

    On Korean-language posts, write the disclosure in plain Korean that names the benefit — '광고', '협찬', '상품 무상 제공' — and put it where the guidelines require: the title or opening for text, inside the image or the first line for photos, and the title or the start and end for video. Use the platform's paid-partnership tools as well, but treat them as an addition, not a replacement.

    For affiliate programs, state in the post that the author earns a commission on purchases, for example: "이 게시물에는 제휴 링크가 포함되어 있으며, 구매 시 일정 수수료가 작성자에게 지급됩니다."

    6. Compliance Workflow for Foreign Brands — 8-Step Pipeline

    Compliance for cross-border brands is a process problem more than a legal problem. Steps 1–4 happen before any asset goes live: draft the brief and creative, get a Korean ad-law review, complete any required category pre-review (medical, health functional food), and fix the disclosure wording and placement in writing. Step 5 is publishing with a dated screenshot of every asset, which becomes evidence if questions come later.

    Steps 6–8 are the part most foreign brands skip: audit comments, creator edits and platform changes after publishing; archive contracts, creative and approvals; and refresh the team whenever the guidelines change. Keep a reviewer log with the reviewer's name, date and the exact disclosure wording, and include indemnification clauses for creator violations in contracts.

    FAQ

    How must influencers disclose sponsored posts in Korea?

    Since December 1, 2024, text posts must disclose the relationship in the title or opening, photo posts inside the image or on the first line, and videos in the title or at the start and end. A disclosure hidden among many hashtags or behind "more" is unlikely to count. Use plain Korean wording that names the benefit, such as 광고, 협찬 or 상품 무상 제공.

    What are the penalties for breaking Korean advertising rules?

    Under the Act on Fair Labeling and Advertising, the KFTC can issue corrective orders and an administrative fine of up to 2% of the related sales (up to ₩500 million where sales cannot be calculated), and a false, deceptive, unfairly comparative or disparaging ad can be punished by up to two years in prison or a fine of up to ₩150 million. In 2019 the KFTC fined seven advertisers a total of ₩269 million over undisclosed Instagram influencer posts.

    Do cosmetics and health supplement ads need pre-approval in Korea?

    Health functional food ads go through prior review by an MFDS-registered self-regulatory body, and their functional claims must match recognized functions. Cosmetics ads do not go through that review, but functional claims such as whitening depend on the product passing MFDS functional-cosmetics review or reporting, and cosmetics may not be advertised as medicines.
